A — Map-Dominant

Model: Spatial situational awareness first. Dispatcher sees where all 20 rides are geographically. Sidebar list acts as secondary index.

Strength: Excellent for spotting geographic clustering (supply gap in north end), surge prediction, and driver availability by zone.

Weakness: Alert escalation gets lost. In alert state, 2 red dots on a map of 20 are easy to miss. Urgent action requires too much scanning. The sidebar must be scrolled to find the right ride.

B — Table-First

Model: Operational efficiency through sortable data. Dispatcher filters to alerts, acts immediately. Mini-map provides spatial context without dominating.

Strength: Best for dispatchers who think in "queue management" — process items in order, mark done, move on. Alert filter zeroes in immediately.

Weakness: In normal state, 20 rows of identical-density data creates uniformity fatigue. Important patterns (long dispatch wait, clustering) aren't visible without active filtering. UI doesn't teach the dispatcher what to look for — reactive, not proactive.

C — Triage Board ★

Model: State-column Kanban. Each ride lives in exactly one column that matches its operational state. The dispatcher's attention flows left-to-right: alerts → searching → matched → in trip → arrived.

Strength: In normal state, the board teaches normal — healthy rides fill the "In Trip" column, sparse "Searching" means good supply. In alert state, a new "Alerts" column materializes at the far left, making the problem spatially impossible to miss. "Searching" column card timers naturally surface oldest waits without filtering.

Key insight: The board communicates system health at a glance via column density — a "Searching" column with 6 cards is visually alarming before any alert fires. This enables proactive dispatch, not just reactive incident response.

Right panel: Mini-map + live KPIs (avg wait, match rate, surge status, drivers available) gives operational context without competing with the action surface.
